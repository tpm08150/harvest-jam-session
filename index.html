Click a lane's name to hear that voice and edit it here. Shift-click plays it without selecting — and records the hit when DR·1 is armed. Verb sends this voice to its own gated reverb and Gate is how long that reverb runs before it is cut — the tail is chopped rather than allowed to ring, which is the whole effect. Every voice has its own, so a huge snare costs the kick nothing. In Program mode these six faders write a parameter lock onto the selected step: a tom that walks down, a hat that opens on the offbeat, a snare that is dry until the fill. Double-click a fader to take its lock off.

Record starts on the next loop boundary, not when you press it — the count-in runs in the bar above. Pick a take first; Record fills the one that is lit.

About the loop

The loop is a fixed number of samples, worked out from the tempo when you armed it. Audio cannot stretch, so changing tempo afterwards means it no longer fits the bar — the tempo it was cut at is shown beside the state. Re-record to move it.

Overdub is a switch, not a moment. Turn it on before or during a take and the first pass rolls straight into layering at the wrap; flip it on while a loop is playing and it punches in where you are, off and it punches out. Neither moves the playhead.

The click is on LP·1's own strip, which is the one strip the looper does not record — so it is audible while you play and never printed into a take. It runs on the shared clock, so starting it with nothing else going makes it the grid everything else then lands on.

Use headphones. An open microphone into speakers records its own output, and an overdub builds that up every pass until it howls. A metronome through speakers into an open microphone is the same problem, and that one the tap cannot save you from.

Arming waits for the next boundary far enough away to give the run-up its full length — a 4-bar riser armed half a bar early lands on the boundary after, not on a half-bar sweep. Now overrides that and takes the very next one. Shape steers the fill as well as the sweep, so a Rise runs the toms upward. Carry lets the sweep hang over into the next part instead of cutting on the downbeat — and the reverb goes with it, so Cut gates the tail off on the boundary the way an 80s room does.
